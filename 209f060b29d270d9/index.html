A self-contained tutorial

PRACBPV from three ingredients

PRACBPV is call-by-push-value (CBPV) with algebraic effects, where every computation runs at a world, and some effects move to a different world: allocating a memory cell, or letting a step of time pass. This page builds its syntax and its semantics from three ingredients.

III · Parametric right adjointsWhy that denotation gives a good typing rule: "pick the operation, then write each continuation in its own context".
IV · Syntax, V · SemanticsThe three put together.
The running program

One small program is followed through every part:

$P$: allocate a new cell holding true, then read it, and continue as $M_{\mathsf t}$ or $M_{\mathsf f}$ depending on what was read.

Each part ends with a box like this one, saying what $P$ is from that part's point of view.

Part 0

Worlds

Programs run at a world; things exist at a world.

A category $\W$ of worlds. A map $w \to w'$ says $w'$ is a possible future of $w$.

Local state: $\W = \Inj$

World $n$ means $n$ allocated cells, $\Fin\,n = \{0, \ldots, n-1\}$. Maps are injections: cells survive and new ones appear.

Guarded: $\W = \omega^{op}$

World $n$ means $n$ steps left. There is a map $n \to m$ when $m \le n$: time runs down.

A presheaf $X : \W \to \Set$ is a type that varies with the world: $X(w)$ is what exists at $w$, and it can be carried into the future. The representable $y\,w$ is "a single point at world $w$".

One fact is used everywhere. A map out of a few points is just an element at each of their worlds:

$$\Psh\Big(\sum_{p} y\,v_p,\ X\Big) \;\cong\; \prod_{p} X(v_p).$$
More precisely

A presheaf here is a functor $X : \W \to \Set$, written covariantly (often called a presheaf on $\W^{op}$). Maps of presheaves are natural transformations. $y\,w = \W[w, -]$, and Yoneda says $\Psh(y\,w, X) \cong X(w)$. The displayed fact is Yoneda plus "a map out of a sum is one map per summand".

Example presheaves: the cells $X(n) = \Fin\,n$ for local state; streams observed for $n$ steps for guarded recursion.

Part I

Parameterized algebraic theories

What an operation looks like.

An effect is given by operations in continuation-passing style: an operation takes one continuation per outcome and runs one of them. Reading a bit: $x \mathbin{?} y$.

Staton's parameterized algebraic theories add two things: operations take parameters (which cell to read), and a continuation may bind new parameters (the cell just allocated).

Local state (Staton 2013), with $\alpha$ the type of cells

$?$a continuation, a cell, a continuationread the cell, continue in one of two branches

PRACBPV keeps this shape of operation, with one change: the binder becomes a lock, a named way in which a continuation's world differs from the current one.

Lock $\fresh$

Behind it there is one more cell, and that cell is available as a parameter.

Lock $\tick$

Behind it there is one step fewer, and nothing new is available.

This part yields: the operation signature

Each operation $o$ has a list of parameter types $\params\,o$, a set of slots $\Ar\,o$ (one per continuation), and for each slot the list of locks its continuation sits behind, its lock word $\slot\,o\,a$. The empty word $\varepsilon$ means "same world".

More precisely: Staton's definition, and why PRACBPV changes the binder

Definition (Staton 2013, Def. 1). Base types $\alpha$; types $\tau ::= \alpha \mid [\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_n]$; function symbols $F : [\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_n]$ where each argument is a parameter $\alpha$ or a continuation $[\alpha^m]$ binding $m$ parameters; equations between terms. Local state: $? : [[\,], \alpha, [\,]]$, $w_i : [\alpha, [\,]]$, $\nu_i : [[\alpha]]$, and a comparison $?_= : [[\,], \alpha, \alpha, [\,]]$.

Staton's binder is an exponential. Models live in presheaves on contexts and renamings $\Ctx_0$, where $[\alpha]X$ is $X(\Gamma, a)$, and renamings may identify variables. So the bound cell might equal an old one, and freshness is an equation: $\nu b.(x(b) \mathrel{?_{a=b}} y(b)) \equiv \nu b.\,y(b)$.

PRACBPV moves freshness into the worlds. Over $\Inj$, "bind a cell" is "move to world $n{+}1$"; the new cell $n$ differs from every old one by construction. PRACBPV also drops equations altogether (operations are lawless; behaviour comes from an abstract machine), and generalizes binders to other locks such as $\tick$. A PAT continuation $[\alpha^m]$ becomes the lock word $\fresh^m$.

$P$ so far

In Staton's syntax: $\nu_{\mathsf t}\big(a.\ ?(M_{\mathsf t}, a, M_{\mathsf f})\big)$. Two operations: new (one slot, behind $\fresh$), and inside it lookup on the bound cell (two slots, no locks).

Part II

Presheaf polynomials

What an operation denotes.

II.1 On sets: shapes and positions

A polynomial functor is a sum of products, $T X = \sum_{s} X^{P(s)}$. The shape $s$ is what an element looks like with its data erased, and the positions $P(s)$ are where data goes.

For plain effects, $T X = \sum_o X^{\Ar\,o}$: a shape is an operation, and its positions are its slots. An element of $T X$ is one layer of a program, an operation with an $X$ in each continuation.

More examples

$X \times X$onetwo

Shapes are always $T\,1$, since erasing the data is mapping $X$ to $1$.

II.2 On presheaves: each position has a world

With worlds, a polynomial says, for each shape, how many positions it has and which world each position lives at, its scope:

$$T X(w) \;=\; \sum_{s} \ \prod_{p} X\big(\sc(w,s,p)\big).$$

By the fact from Part 0, this is the same as giving each shape an arity, the few points $\sum_p y(\sc(w,s,p))$, "one point per position, at its world". A map out of the arity is exactly one $X$ per position. (Why this rewriting matters is derived in detail in III.2.)

The base types also become presheaves: $\Const_b(w)$ is the values of type $b$ at world $w$. For cells, $\Const_{\Lc}(n) = \Fin\,n$.

More precisely: functoriality, families of worlds, and the polynomial in Cat

Shapes form a presheaf $S$. Along $f : w \to w'$, shapes go forward; positions go backwards, $\mathrm{pos}_f : \Pos(w', S(f)s) \to \Pos(w, s)$; and scopes go forward, $\mathrm{smap}_f(p') : \sc(w, s, \mathrm{pos}_f\,p') \to \sc(w', S(f)s, p')$. Positions go backwards because to fill a position $p'$ at $w'$ you fetch the $X$ at position $\mathrm{pos}_f\,p'$ and carry it forward along $\mathrm{smap}_f\,p'$.

A family of worlds (a set $I$ and a world for each $i$) is an object of $\Fam(\W)$, the free coproduct completion of $\W^{op}$; its morphisms are exactly presheaf maps between sums of representables. Positions and scopes together are one functor $E : (\int S)^{op} \to \Fam(\W)$, and $T X(w) \cong \sum_s \Psh(E(w,s), X)$: a family of representable functors ("familially representable").

For category theorists: this is a polynomial in $\mathbf{Cat}$, $\W \leftarrow \int\!\Pos \to \int\! S \to \W$ with a discrete opfibration and a discrete fibration; $T$ is pullback, then products along the first, then sums along the second. In the code: Poly, and G : Functor (∫ Shapes) (Fam W) in Polynomial.agda.

II.3 A lock is a one-shape polynomial

A lock kind $\kappa$ has one shape, "the continuation". Its positions $\Pos_\kappa(w)$ are the ways of crossing the lock at $w$, and their scopes $\sc_\kappa(w,p)$ are the worlds on the far side. Its polynomial is the modality

$$R_\kappa X(w) \;=\; \prod_{p \in \Pos_\kappa(w)} X\big(\sc_\kappa(w,p)\big),$$

"an $X$ on the far side of the lock".

A lock kind also carries two pieces of data that are not part of the polynomial: a strength, saying the far side is a future of the near side (so old things can be carried across), and its names, the new parameters available on the far side.

$\fresh$

One position; far side $n{+}1$. $R_{\fresh} X(n) = X(n{+}1)$.

Strength: $n \hookrightarrow n{+}1$. Name: the new cell $n$.

$\tick$

No position at stage 0; one at $n{+}1$, far side $n$. $R_{\tick} X(0) = 1$ and $R_{\tick} X(n{+}1) = X(n)$, the later modality $\triangleright$.

Strength: $n{+}1 \to n$. No names.

More precisely: the five pieces of data, and a lock with several positions
  1. Positions $\Pos_\kappa(w)$.
  2. Scopes $\sc_\kappa(w,p)$.
  3. Functoriality $\mathrm{pos}_f$, $\mathrm{smap}_f$ as in II.2. Items 1–3 together are a functor $E_\kappa : \W^{op} \to \Fam(\W)$.
  4. Strength $\iota_{w,p} : w \to \sc_\kappa(w,p)$, natural in $w$.
  5. Names: base types $\vec b_\kappa$ and generic values $\gen_{w,p} \in \prod_b \Const_b(\sc_\kappa(w,p))$, natural in $w$.

For $\fresh$, $\mathrm{smap}_f = f{+}1$ (old cells by $f$, new cell to new cell). A lock with several positions (not in the development): Staton's exponential binder over $\Inj$ is the lock kind with $\Pos(n) = 1 + \Fin\,n$, scope $n{+}1$ for "new" and $n$ for "equal to cell $i$", since $y\,n \times \Const_{\Lc} \cong y(n{+}1) + n\cdot y\,n$. Its case split is what Staton's $?_=$ does by equations.

II.4 Locks in a row, and the whole signature

A lock word is crossed one lock at a time: its positions are sequences of choices, and its far side is where the last lock lands. Then all the operations together form one polynomial, the signature functor:

$$\Ext\,X(w) \;=\; \sum_{o} \ \sum_{\text{params}} \ \prod_{a \in \Ar\,o} \ \prod_{\text{ways across } \slot\,o\,a} X(\text{far side}).$$

One layer of a program: an operation, its parameter values, and for each continuation an $X$ at the world its lock word leads to.

Local state ($\W = \Inj$)

Three families of operations: lookup (one cell, two slots, no locks), update$_b$ for $b \in \mathsf{Bool}$ (one cell, one slot, no lock), and new$_b$ (no parameters, one slot behind $\fresh$). Plugging them into the formula for $\Ext$:

$$\Ext\,X(n) \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_{i \in \Fin n} X(n) \times X(n)}_{\text{lookup cell } i} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{b \in \mathsf{Bool}} \sum_{i \in \Fin n} X(n)}_{\text{update}_b \text{ cell } i} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{b \in \mathsf{Bool}} X(n{+}1)}_{\text{new}_b}$$

So $\Ext\,1(n) = \Fin\,n + 2 \cdot \Fin\,n + 2$, which has $3n + 2$ elements. Along an injection $f : n \hookrightarrow m$: a lookup or update of cell $i$ becomes one of cell $f(i)$, with its continuations carried along $f$; a new$_b$ keeps its shape, with its continuation carried along $f{+}1$ (old cells by $f$, new cell to new cell).

Guarded recursion ($\W = \omega^{op}$)

One operation, step: no parameters, one slot behind $\tick$. So there is one shape at every stage, and

$$\Ext\,X(0) \;=\; 1, \qquad \Ext\,X(n{+}1) \;=\; X(n), \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \Ext \;=\; \triangleright.$$

The signature functor is the later modality. At stage 0 the only element is the step with no continuation ($\Psh(0, X) = 1$); at stage $n{+}1$ an element is a step together with what happens at stage $n$. With one shape, $\Ext\,1 = 1$, so here the signature functor is an honest right adjoint, its left adjoint being "earlier", $\triangleleft$.

For comparison: no worlds

With $\W$ the one-object category and no lock kinds (the POPL setting), $\Ext\,X = \sum_o X^{\Ar\,o}$, e.g. $\Ext\,X = 1$ for a single error operation with no slots, or $\Ext\,X = X \times X + X + X$ for global boolean state (get with two slots, put$_0$, put$_1$).

Part III

Parametric right adjoints

Why these polynomials give good typing rules.

III.1 The idea

A right adjoint $R$ has a partner $L$ with maps into $R X$ ≅ maps out of $L\,\Gamma$. That is exactly the form of an introduction rule: "to build an $R X$ from $\Gamma$, build an $X$ in the bigger context $L\Gamma$".

Polynomials with many shapes are not right adjoints, but they are the next best thing: a parametric right adjoint (PRA). To map into $T X$, first choose a shape from $\Gamma$; then, for that shape, you are mapping into a right adjoint.

$$\text{maps } \Gamma \to T X \;\;=\;\; \text{a shape } s \text{ chosen from } \Gamma \;\;+\;\; \text{a map } L(\Gamma, s) \to X.$$

$L(\Gamma, s)$ is "$\Gamma$ unpacked along the positions of $s$". On a single point it gives the arity of $s$. A lock kind has one shape, so its modality $R_\kappa$ is an honest right adjoint; its partner $L_\kappa$ is what a lock in a context will mean.

More precisely: the definition

Let $\mathcal A$ have a terminal object and $T : \mathcal A \to \mathcal B$. Each $T X$ maps to $T\,1$ (send every element to its shape), so $T$ factors as $\mathcal A \xrightarrow{T_1} \mathcal B/T1 \xrightarrow{\Sigma} \mathcal B$ with $T_1 X = (T X, T(!))$. $T$ is a PRA if $T_1$ is a right adjoint (Weber; nLab). When $T1 = 1$ this is just a right adjoint. On sets, for $T X = \sum_s X^{P(s)}$, the left adjoint sends a point of shape $s$ to $P(s)$.

III.2 PRAs and polynomials are the same thing

On presheaves, a functor is a PRA exactly when it is a polynomial in the sense of Part II. The polynomial is the data; being a PRA is its universal property.

Why, in one line each way. A polynomial is a PRA because, once the shape is fixed, maps into $T X$ are maps out of the arity. A PRA is a polynomial because every element of a given shape is a renaming of one generic element, whose positions hold their own names, and those positions are the polynomial's positions.

A generic element

For the shape "lookup cell $i$" at world $n$, the generic element is "read cell $i$, then continue at point 1 or at point 2". Every concrete read of cell $i$ is this, with point 1 and point 2 renamed to the actual continuations.

In particular, the formula $T X(w) \cong \sum_s \Psh(E(w,s), X)$ from II.2 is this property in disguise: once the shape is chosen, maps into $T X$ are maps out of the arity.

Derivation, step by step: why $T X(w) \cong \sum_s \Psh(E(w,s), X)$, and why that formula is the PRA property

The question. Part II wrote a polynomial in two ways:

$$T X(w) \;=\; \sum_{s \in S(w)} \ \prod_{p \in \Pos(w,s)} X\big(\sc(w,s,p)\big) \qquad\text{and}\qquad T X(w) \;\cong\; \sum_{s \in S(w)} \Psh\big(E(w,s),\, X\big), \quad E(w,s) = \sum_{p} y\big(\sc(w,s,p)\big).$$

Why rewrite the first as the second? It is not a choice made to fit the definition of PRA. The two are isomorphic for every polynomial, by two standard facts. But the second form is the PRA property, written out. The steps below show both points.

Step 1: one position is one map out of one point (Yoneda)

Fix a world $v$ and a presheaf $X$. There is a bijection

$$X(v) \;\cong\; \Psh(y\,v,\ X).$$
  • From left to right. An element $x \in X(v)$ gives the map $\hat x : y\,v \to X$ which, at a world $t$, sends $g : v \to t$ (an element of $y\,v$ at $t$) to $X(g)(x)$: "carry $x$ forward along $g$". This is natural because carrying forward twice is carrying forward along the composite.
  • From right to left. A map $\varphi : y\,v \to X$ gives the element $\varphi_v(\mathrm{id}_v) \in X(v)$: "where the identity goes".
  • Why these are inverse. Naturality of $\varphi$ forces $\varphi_t(g) = X(g)\big(\varphi_v(\mathrm{id}_v)\big)$ for every $g : v \to t$, because $g = \mathrm{id}_v$ carried forward along $g$. So $\varphi$ is determined by where $\mathrm{id}_v$ goes. Conversely $\hat x$ sends $\mathrm{id}_v$ to $x$.

In words: a single point at world $v$ can be mapped into $X$ in exactly as many ways as $X$ has elements at $v$.

Step 2: several positions are one map out of several points

Sums of presheaves are computed world by world: $\big(\sum_p A_p\big)(t) = \sum_p A_p(t)$, a disjoint union. So a map out of a sum is one map out of each summand, and nothing more:

$$\prod_{p} \Psh(A_p, X) \;\cong\; \Psh\Big(\sum_p A_p,\ X\Big).$$

From left to right, combine a family of maps $(\varphi_p)_p$ into one map that uses $\varphi_p$ on the $p$-th summand ("copairing"). From right to left, restrict a map to each summand.

Step 3: put them together, for one shape

Fix a shape $s$ at $w$, and write $\sc_p$ for $\sc(w,s,p)$:

$$\prod_{p} X(\sc_p) \ \overset{\text{Step 1}}{\cong}\ \prod_p \Psh(y\,\sc_p,\ X) \ \overset{\text{Step 2}}{\cong}\ \Psh\Big(\sum_p y\,\sc_p,\ X\Big) \;=\; \Psh\big(E(w,s),\ X\big).$$

Explicitly: a tuple $(x_p)_p$, with $x_p \in X(\sc_p)$, corresponds to the map $E(w,s) \to X$ that sends an element $(p, g)$ of $E(w,s)$ at world $t$, meaning "position $p$, then $g : \sc_p \to t$", to $X(g)(x_p)$.

The arity $E(w,s)$ is the "pattern" of the shape: one point per position, each at its own world. Filling the pattern with data from $X$ is the same as mapping the pattern into $X$.

Step 4: sum over shapes

Do Step 3 for every shape and take the sum:

$$T X(w) \;=\; \sum_{s \in S(w)} \prod_p X(\sc_p) \;\cong\; \sum_{s \in S(w)} \Psh\big(E(w,s),\ X\big).$$

That is the formula. So far only Yoneda and coproducts have been used: every polynomial can be rewritten this way, so there is nothing to "make compatible".

Step 5: it is natural in $X$, so $T$ is a sum of representable functors

For a map $h : X \to X'$, the left side acts by applying $h$ at every position, $(x_p)_p \mapsto (h(x_p))_p$. The right side acts by postcomposition, $\varphi \mapsto h \circ \varphi$. Under Step 3 these agree, because $h$ commutes with carrying forward: $h(X(g)(x_p)) = X'(g)(h(x_p))$. So, as functors of $X$,

$$T(-)(w) \;\cong\; \sum_{s \in S(w)} \Psh\big(E(w,s),\ -\big).$$

Each summand $\Psh(E(w,s), -)$ is a representable functor: everything about it is determined by one object, $E(w,s)$. A sum of representable functors is what "familially representable" means: a family of representables, indexed by shapes.

Step 6: it is natural in $w$, and the action is precomposition

Let $f : w \to w'$ and $s' = S(f)(s)$. The arities are related by a presheaf map going the other way,

$$E(f) : E(w', s') \to E(w, s), \qquad \text{summand } p' \ \mapsto\ \text{summand } \mathrm{pos}_f\,p', \text{ along } y(\mathrm{smap}_f\,p').$$

On the right-hand side, $T X(f)$ sends $(s, \varphi)$ to $(s', \varphi \circ E(f))$: "keep the data, re-read it through the new pattern". Unfolding at a position $p'$ of $s'$, $\varphi \circ E(f)$ looks up the data at position $\mathrm{pos}_f\,p'$ and carries it forward along $\mathrm{smap}_f\,p'$:

$$\big(T X(f)(s, x)\big)_{p'} \;=\; X(\mathrm{smap}_f\,p')\big(x_{\mathrm{pos}_f\,p'}\big).$$

That is exactly the action written in the product form. So the two forms agree as presheaves in $w$ too, and in the second form all the functoriality of positions and scopes is packed into one thing: precomposition with $E(f)$.

Step 7: why this formula is the PRA property

First, the shapes really are $T\,1$. Taking $X = 1$, every $\Psh(E(w,s), 1)$ is a single point, so $T\,1(w) \cong \sum_s 1 = S(w)$. The map $T(!) : T X \to T\,1$ sends $(s, \varphi)$ to $s$: it forgets the contents and keeps the shape.

Now recall the definition (III.1): $T$ is a PRA when $T_1 : X \mapsto (T X, T(!))$, a functor into the slice $\Psh/T1$, is a right adjoint. The simplest objects of that slice are a single point at $w$ labelled by a shape, $(y\,w,\ s)$ with $s \in T1(w)$. A map in the slice from $(y\,w, s)$ into $T_1 X$ is a map $y\,w \to T X$ whose label is $s$. By Yoneda that is an element of $T X(w)$ of shape $s$, and by Step 4 the elements of shape $s$ are the summand $\Psh(E(w,s), X)$. So:

$$\big(\Psh/T1\big)\big((y\,w, s),\ T_1 X\big) \;\cong\; \Psh\big(E(w,s),\ X\big), \quad\text{naturally in } X.$$

Read right to left, this is the defining property of a left adjoint, $L(y\,w, s) := E(w,s)$, on these simplest objects. Every object of the slice is glued (a colimit) from such labelled points, and a left adjoint must preserve gluing, so this determines $L$ everywhere, and it exists. That is: $T_1$ is a right adjoint, so $T$ is a PRA, and its left adjoint sends a labelled point to the arity of the label.

So the formula does not make $T$ PRA-compatible. It is the PRA property, stated on single points, and the arity is the left adjoint applied to a point.

Step 8: the converse, and why the second form is the more general one

Run Step 7 backwards. If $T$ is any PRA, define $E(w,s) := L(y\,w, s)$, the left adjoint on a labelled point, and Yoneda gives $T X(w) \cong \sum_s \Psh(E(w,s), X)$ again. But now $E(w,s)$ can be any presheaf. Only when it happens to be a sum of representables, $\sum_p y(\sc_p)$, does Step 3 run backwards to give the product form with positions and scopes. So:

  • $\sum_s \Psh(E(w,s), X)$ describes every PRA;
  • $\sum_s \prod_p X(\sc_p)$ describes the PRAs whose arities are sums of representables (familial arities).

PRACBPV restricts to the second on purpose. Because each arity is a sum of single points, "behind the lock, crossed in way $p$" is a single world, $\sc_p$, and that is what makes contexts know their world (IV.2).

Why the second form is the one to work with

  1. Generality. It describes every PRA (Step 8).
  2. It has the shape of a typing rule. "Choose a shape, then give a map out of the arity" becomes "choose the operation and its parameters, then write each continuation in the context the arity describes". The arity is a presheaf, so it can be what a context means: the context behind the lock.
  3. Naturality is one operation. Moving between worlds is precomposition with $E(f)$ (Step 6), instead of tracking positions backwards and scopes forwards by hand.
More precisely: the theorem, familial arities, and a caveat

Theorem (Weber, Prop. 2.10 and Rem. 2.12, as cited on nLab; nLab Prop. 3.3). For a functor between presheaf categories, the following are equivalent: it is a PRA; every element of $T1$ at a representable has a generic factorization; it is a polynomial in $\mathbf{Cat}$, with arities $E(w,s) = L(y\,w, s)$.

A general PRA may have any presheaf as an arity. Ours have sums of representables (familial arities), which is what makes "behind a lock, in a given way" a single world.

Caveat: "polynomial" means a polynomial in $\mathbf{Cat}$, not an internal one in the presheaf topos. $R_{\fresh}$ is not an internal exponential $X^A$: that would need $y\,n \times A \cong y(n{+}1)$, forcing $A = y\,1$ at $n = 0$, but $y\,1 \times y\,1 \not\cong y\,2$. The same goes for $\triangleright$.

III.3 The lock $L_\kappa$

Behind a $\kappa$-lock placed right after a single point at world $w$, you are simply at one of the far-side worlds:

$$L_\kappa(y\,w) \;\cong\; \sum_{p \in \Pos_\kappa(w)} y\big(\sc_\kappa(w,p)\big).$$

On a general context, $L_\kappa$ applies this to every point, so an element behind the lock carries the way $p$ it crossed. Grouping by $p$ splits $L_\kappa\Gamma$ into summands, one per way of crossing, and each summand sits over a single world. Part IV's contexts are built on this.

$\fresh$

$L(y\,n) \cong y(n{+}1)$. In general, $L\Gamma$ is "$\Gamma$ plus one cell that $\Gamma$ does not mention".

$\tick$

$(L\Gamma)(n) = \Gamma(n{+}1)$: behind the lock you hold an environment from one step earlier. At stage 0 nothing is behind the lock.

More precisely: the formula for $L_\kappa$ and the adjunction
$$L_\kappa \Gamma(t) = \Big(\sum_{v} \sum_{q \in \Pos_\kappa(v)} \Gamma(v) \times \W\big[\sc_\kappa(v,q), t\big]\Big) \Big/ \sim$$

An element: an environment $e$ at an earlier world $v$, a way $q$ of crossing there, and a way to continue from the far side to $t$; $\sim$ identifies moving $e$ forward before or after crossing. The two transposes: $\psi : L_\kappa\Gamma \to X$ gives $e \mapsto (q \mapsto \psi\langle v,q,e,\mathrm{id}\rangle)$; $\varphi : \Gamma \to R_\kappa X$ gives $\langle v,q,e,g\rangle \mapsto X(g)(\varphi(e)(q))$.

Summands. If $\Gamma$ comes with $\pi : \Gamma \to y\,w$ ("lies over $w$"), then $L_\kappa\pi : L_\kappa\Gamma \to \sum_p y(\sc_\kappa(w,p))$ labels each element with a position; the $p$-th summand lies over $\sc_\kappa(w,p)$. Example: $L_{\fresh}(\Const_{\Lc}) \cong y\,2$, pairs of distinct cells.

III.4 From the universal property to typing rules

Each universal property becomes a rule:

Right adjoint ⟹ lock rules
$$\frac{\Gamma, 🔒 \vdash t : A}{\Gamma \vdash \shut\,t : [\kappa]A} \qquad \frac{\Gamma \vdash t : [\kappa]A}{\Gamma, 🔒 \vdash \opn\,t : A}$$

The context $\Gamma, 🔒$ means $L_\kappa\Gamma$; $\shut$ is the bijection, $\opn$ undoes it.

Many-shape PRA ⟹ the operation rule

Choose the shape from $\Gamma$ (an operation and its parameters); then write each continuation in the context $L(\Gamma, \text{shape})$, i.e. behind its slot's locks.

More precisely: the lock is a PRA too, and substitution (Gratzer et al. 2022)

Pushing a substitution through $\opn$ needs the lock functor on contexts, $\Gamma \mapsto \Gamma, 🔒$, to be a PRA itself. Its shapes are keys $r : \Delta \to 1, 🔒$ (in the pure syntax: a choice of one lock in $\Delta$), and a substitution $\Delta \to \Gamma, 🔒$ is a key plus a substitution from "$\Delta$ cut off at that lock" to $\Gamma$. This is the rule style of Clouston and of Birkedal et al., made substitution-stable by Gratzer, Cavallo, Kavvos, Guatto and Birkedal.

$P$ so far

Writing $P$ means: choose the shape new$_{\mathsf t}$; then write its continuation behind a $\fresh$ lock, where one cell exists. There, choose the shape lookup of that cell, and write two continuations in the same context.

Later: $\Gamma, 🔒$ means $\Gamma(n{+}1)$. Fresh names: $\Gamma$ plus a name it does not use. Interval: worlds are dimensions of cubes, $\Gamma, 🔒$ means $\Gamma \times \I$. $\up$ needs a map $L\Gamma \to \Gamma$; $\nm$ needs a global element of $R\,N$.

Lesson. If $\opn$, $\up$, $\nm$ only apply when the lock is last in the context, then $\Gamma, 🔒, x{:}B \vdash \opn\,t$ is not derivable: "bind $a$ in $\lambda x.\,(a, x)$" cannot be written and substitution fails. The fix is to say which lock a rule crosses, and to let substitutions into $\Gamma, 🔒$ record which lock they go through: the key decomposition above. Part IV

The syntax

The three ingredients assembled.

IV.1 Types

CBPV's value types $A$ and computation types $B$, plus base types and one modality per lock kind:

$$A ::= \mathbb 1 \mid A \times A \mid U B \mid \mathsf{base}\ b \mid [\kappa] A \qquad\qquad B ::= F A \mid A \Rightarrow B$$

$U B$ is a suspended computation and $F A$ a computation returning an $A$. Operations get no type; they appear only in the operation rule.

IV.2 Contexts know their world

A context starts at a world and records each lock it crosses, so it always knows the current world:

$$\Theta ::= \varnothing_w \mid \Theta, A \mid \Theta \lockat{p}{\kappa} \qquad\quad \wd(\varnothing_w) = w, \quad \wd(\Theta \lockat{p}{\kappa}) = \sc_\kappa(\wd\,\Theta, p)$$

Variables don't change the world. A lock records which way $p$ it was crossed, so its far side is one world: this is the summand of III.3. With no locks, a context is the POPL paper's $w \mid \Gamma$.

Local state

$\varnothing_0 \lockat{}{\fresh}$ is at world 1: one cell has been allocated.

Guarded

$\varnothing_1 \lockat{}{\tick}$ is at stage 0; no further tick can be written, as there is no way to cross.

More precisely: telescopes, and how they differ from the literature

Contexts are telescopes, defined together with $\wd$ (a position is only meaningful at the world reached so far). $\varnothing_w$ denotes $y\,w$; $\Theta, A$ denotes a product; $\Theta \lockat{p}{\kappa}$ denotes the $p$-th summand of $L_\kappa\den\Theta$. For a lock word, $\Theta \lockat{\vec p}{\mu}$ appends its locks one at a time.

Both differences are deliberate: programs run at a world (as in the POPL paper), and splitting $L_\kappa$ by position keeps every context over one world. In the instances the second barely shows: with one way to cross, the summand is all of $L_\kappa\Theta$; with none ($\tick$ at stage 0), the context simply cannot be formed. The payoff is closing: $\varnothing_u \lockat{p}{\kappa}$ is the same as $\varnothing_{\sc_\kappa(u,p)}$, which the machine uses after every operation.

IV.3 Values

The CBPV values, plus the lock rules of III.4, now crossing a lock at a recorded position:

$$\frac{\Delta \vdash n : A}{\Delta \lockat{p}{\kappa} \vdash \up\,n : A} \quad \frac{\Delta \vdash n : [\kappa]A}{\Delta \lockat{p}{\kappa} \vdash \opn\,n : A} \quad \frac{}{\Delta \lockat{p}{\kappa} \vdash \nm_i : \mathsf{base}\ b_i} \quad \frac{c \in \Const_b(\wd\,\Theta)}{\Theta \vdash \mathsf{const}\ c : \mathsf{base}\ b}$$ $$\frac{\text{for each way } p \text{ of crossing}:\ \ \Theta \lockat{p}{\kappa} \vdash V_p : A}{\Theta \vdash \shut\,(V_p)_p : [\kappa]A}$$

$\shut$ needs one value per way of crossing, because $R_\kappa$ is a product over them. For $\fresh$ that is one value; for $\tick$ at stage 0 it is none.

More precisely: where each rule comes from

$\up$, $\opn$ and $\nm$ are neutrals (built from the context), each crossing the last lock: $\up$ uses the strength, $\opn$ the counit of $L_\kappa \dashv R_\kappa$, $\nm_i$ the lock's names. $\mathsf{const}$ uses $\Const_b$ at the current world. In $\varnothing_0 \lockat{}{\fresh}$, $\nm_0$ ("the cell this lock made") and $\mathsf{const}\ 0$ ("cell 0") are different syntax for the same cell; closing turns the first into the second.

More precisely: renaming, substitution and closing

Renamings and substitutions are data. keepL goes under a lock ($L_\kappa$ is a functor); dropL crosses one (the strength); lockₛ is the key decomposition of III.4, used to push a substitution past $\opn$; close is closing (IV.2). IV.5 Derived rules for the instances

Each instance defines its familiar operations from the general rules. Their typing rules follow, each with its one-line definition underneath.

Local state ($\Lc = \mathsf{base}\ \Lc$; $\Theta \lockat{}{\fresh}$ written $\Theta \# \ell$)

Cells

$$\frac{i \in \Fin(\wd\,\Theta)}{\Theta \vdash \mathsf{cell}\ i : \Lc} \qquad\qquad \frac{}{\Delta \# \ell \vdash \ell : \Lc}$$

$\mathsf{cell}\ i = \mathsf{const}\ i$: an existing cell. $\ell = \nm_0$: the cell allocated by the nearest $\fresh$ lock.

Reading and writing

$$\frac{\Theta \vdash V : \Lc \qquad \Theta \vdash M : B \qquad \Theta \vdash N : B}{\Theta \vdash \mathsf{read}\ V\ M\ N : B} \qquad\qquad \frac{\Theta \vdash V : \Lc \qquad b \in \mathsf{Bool} \qquad \Theta \vdash M : B}{\Theta \vdash \mathsf{write}\ V\ b\ M : B}$$

$\mathsf{read}\ V\ M\ N = \op\ \mathsf{lookup}\ [V]\ (\mathsf{true} \mapsto M;\ \mathsf{false} \mapsto N)$ and $\mathsf{write}\ V\ b\ M = \op\ \mathsf{update}_b\ [V]\ (\ast \mapsto M)$. Neither has a lock, so the continuations stay in $\Theta$.

Allocation

$$\frac{\Theta \# \ell \vdash M : B}{\Theta \vdash \mathsf{new}\ell\ b\ M : B} \qquad\qquad \frac{\Theta,\ x{:}\Lc \vdash M : B}{\Theta \vdash \mathsf{new}x\ b\ M : B}$$

$\mathsf{new}\ell\ b\ M = \op\ \mathsf{new}_b\ [\,]\ (\ast \mapsto M)$: the continuation lives behind the lock, where $\ell$ is the new cell. $\mathsf{new}x$ is the familiar "$\mathsf{new}\ x := b\ \mathsf{in}\ M$" with an ordinary variable: it is $\mathsf{new}\ell$ applied to $M$ with $\ell$ substituted for $x$, the rest of $\Theta$ moved across the lock with $\up$.

The modality $\Lc \multimap A = [\fresh]A$

$$\frac{\Theta \# \ell \vdash V : A}{\Theta \vdash \shut\,V : [\fresh]A} \qquad\qquad \frac{\Theta \vdash V : [\fresh]A}{\Theta \# \ell \vdash \mathsf{open}\ V : A} \qquad\qquad \frac{}{\Theta \vdash \mathsf{generic} : [\fresh]\Lc}$$

$\fresh$ has exactly one way across, so $\shut$ takes one value. $\mathsf{open}$ (openV) is $\opn$ extended to all values: it returns the body of a $\shut$ directly. $\mathsf{generic} = \shut\,\ell$: "the cell you will get when you allocate".

Guarded ($\triangleright A = [\tick]A$; $\Theta \lockat{}{\tick}$ written $\Theta, \checkmark$)

The modality

$$\frac{\wd\,\Theta = n{+}1 \qquad \Theta, \checkmark \vdash V : A}{\Theta \vdash \shut\,V : \triangleright A} \qquad\qquad \frac{\wd\,\Theta = 0}{\Theta \vdash \shut\,() : \triangleright A} \qquad\qquad \frac{\Theta \vdash V : \triangleright A}{\Theta, \checkmark \vdash \mathsf{open}\ V : A}$$

$\shut$ takes one value per way across the tick: one at stage $n{+}1$, none at stage 0. At stage 0 every $\triangleright A$ is the trivial $\shut\,()$, and there is no context $\Theta, \checkmark$ in which to open it.

next and ⊛

$$\frac{\Theta \vdash V : A}{\Theta \vdash \mathsf{next}\ V : \triangleright A} \qquad\qquad \frac{\Theta \vdash f : \triangleright U(A \Rightarrow B) \qquad \Theta \vdash x : \triangleright A}{\Theta \vdash f \circledast x : \triangleright U B}$$

$\mathsf{next}\ V = \shut\,(\up\ V)$: what you have now, you also have later (box tick). $f \circledast x = \shut\,\big(\mathsf{thunk}\,(\mathsf{app}\,(\mathsf{force}\,(\mathsf{open}\,f))\,(\mathsf{open}\,x))\big)$: open both behind one tick and apply.

Taking a step

$$\frac{\Theta \vdash M : B}{\Theta \vdash \mathsf{delay}\ M : B} \qquad\qquad \frac{\Theta \vdash V : \triangleright U B}{\Theta \vdash \mathsf{later}\ V : B}$$

$\mathsf{delay}\ M = \op\ \mathsf{step}\ [\,]\ (p \mapsto \up\ M)$: take a step, then run $M$ one stage later (the whole of $M$ moved across the tick). $\mathsf{later}\ V = \op\ \mathsf{step}\ [\,]\ (p \mapsto \mathsf{force}\,(\mathsf{open}\ V))$: take a step, then run the computation that was promised for later. Neither rule needs a side condition: at stage 0, $\mathsf{step}$ has no way across, so both become $\op\ \mathsf{step}\ [\,]\ ()$, a program that has run out of time.

Part V

The semantics

Each piece of syntax, interpreted with the structure it came from.

Value types denote presheaves. Computation types denote $\Ext$-algebras: presheaves that know how to perform an operation given its continuations. $F A$ is the free one, trees of operations with leaves in $A$.

syntaxdenotation
$\mathsf{base}\ b$$\Const_b$
$[\kappa]A$$R_\kappa\den A$
$F A$the free $\Ext$-algebra on $\den A$
context $\varnothing_w$$y\,w$, a point at $w$
lock $\Theta \lockat{p}{\kappa}$the $p$-summand of $L_\kappa\den\Theta$
$\shut$, $\opn$the adjunction, forwards and backwards
$\op\ o\ \vec V\ k$package the shape and the continuations as an element of $\Ext\,\den B$, then let $\den B$ perform it

The operation clause in three steps:

$$\den{\op\ o\ \vec V\ k} \;=\; \den\Theta \xrightarrow{\ \text{shape and continuations}\ } \Ext\,\den B \xrightarrow{\ \text{perform}\ } \den B$$
  1. Each continuation is moved across its lock word: this is $\shut$, once per lock.
  2. Paired with the shape, they form one layer, an element of $\Ext\,\den B$.
  3. The algebra $\den B$ performs it.

With no locks, step 1 does nothing: this is the POPL paper's semantics of operations.

More precisely: the full table, and the names in the code

$\mathbb 1$, $\times$ pointwise; $U B$ the underlying presheaf of $\den B$; $A \Rightarrow B$ the presheaf exponential, an algebra via the strength. Each context lies over its world. A value denotes a map $\den\Theta \to \den A$.

Maps out of a lock are unlocks (transposes back along $L_\kappa \dashv R_\kappa$): $\up\,n$ is $\den n$ after the strength map $L_\kappa\Gamma \to \Gamma$; $\opn\,n$ is the unlock of $\den n$ (the counit); $\nm_i$ is the unlock of the names; $\mathsf{const}\ c$ goes through $y(\wd\,\Theta)$ by Yoneda; $\shut\,(V_p)_p$ copairs the $\den{V_p}$ over the summands and transposes.

In Semantics/Denotation.agda: step 1 is shutW, step 2 opNode, step 3 the algebra of ⟦ B ⟧c.

$P$, finally

At world 0, $\den P$ (in $F A$) is a tree: a node new$_{\mathsf t}$, whose single child lives at world 1 and is a node "lookup cell 0" with two children, $\den{M_{\mathsf t}}$ and $\den{M_{\mathsf f}}$ at world 1. The operational semantics runs it: allocate (moving to world 1 by closing), read cell 0, find true, continue as $M_{\mathsf t}$.
