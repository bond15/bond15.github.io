1. Products and sums as data

In types, 1 and 0 now mean unit and Empty_set; in programs they are still the numbers 1 and 0.

Sums appear in many languages. \(\tau_1 + \tau_2\) is Either[A, B] in Scala, Result<T, E> in Rust, and Either a b in Haskell. In each, \(\mathsf{in}_1\) and \(\mathsf{in}_2\) are Left and Right (Scala, Haskell) or Ok and Err (Rust).

\(\mathbf{0}\) has no introduction form, so it has no values, and a case on it has no branches: this is the lecture's \(\mathsf{case}_{\mathbf{0}}\ e\ \{\}\). From an impossible value, you can return anything.

Counting values

For types with finitely many values, write \(|\tau|\) for how many values \(\tau\) has. A pair picks one of each, a sum picks one side, and a function picks an output for each input:

\[ \begin{gathered} |\tau_1 \times \tau_2| = |\tau_1| \cdot |\tau_2| \qquad |\tau_1 + \tau_2| = |\tau_1| + |\tau_2| \qquad |\tau_1 \to \tau_2| = |\tau_2|^{|\tau_1|} \\ |\mathbf{1}| = 1 \qquad |\mathbf{0}| = 0 \end{gathered} \]

This is why they are called algebraic data types. For example, \(\mathsf{bool} \times \mathsf{bool}\) has \(2 \cdot 2 = 4\) values, and \(\mathbf{1} + \mathbf{1}\) has \(1 + 1 = 2\), as many as \(\mathsf{bool}\). That is why \(\mathsf{bool}\) is also written \(\mathbf{2}\).

2. Isomorphisms

Two types are isomorphic, \(\tau_1 \cong \tau_2\), when there are programs \(f : \tau_1 \to \tau_2\) and \(g : \tau_2 \to \tau_1\) that undo each other:

\[ g\,(f\, x) = x \text{ for every } x : \tau_1, \qquad f\,(g\, y) = y \text{ for every } y : \tau_2 \]

Then \(\tau_1\) and \(\tau_2\) have the same values, with different names.

Each isomorphism below is a pair of programs \(f\) and \(g\) in the lecture's language. Try writing each program from its type before reading it. In Rocq, each pair sits in a module of its own, so every one can be called f and g; from outside, they are ProdUnit.f, Dist.g, and so on. In Rocq, the proof that the two programs undo each other always has the same shape: split into the two directions; in each, take the value apart, one case per way it can be built; then each case computes, and reflexivity finishes it.

\(\tau \times \mathbf{1} \cong \tau\)

Like \(n \cdot 1 = n\). The second part of the pair can only be \(()\), so it carries no information.

\[ \begin{aligned} f &: \tau \times \mathbf{1} \to \tau & f &= \lambda p{:}\tau \times \mathbf{1}.\, p.1 \\ g &: \tau \to \tau \times \mathbf{1} & g &= \lambda x{:}\tau.\, (x, ()) \end{aligned} \]

\(\tau + \mathbf{0} \cong \tau\)

Like \(n + 0 = n\). The \(\mathsf{in}_2\) side would hold a value of \(\mathbf{0}\), which can't exist, so only \(\mathsf{in}_1\) ever happens.

\[ \begin{aligned} f &: \tau + \mathbf{0} \to \tau & f &= \lambda s{:}\tau + \mathbf{0}.\, \mathsf{case}_+\ s\ \{\mathsf{in}_1\ x \to x \mid \mathsf{in}_2\ z \to \mathsf{case}_{\mathbf{0}}\ z\ \{\}\} \\ g &: \tau \to \tau + \mathbf{0} & g &= \lambda x{:}\tau.\, \mathsf{in}_1\ x \end{aligned} \]

\(\tau \times \mathbf{0} \cong \mathbf{0}\)

Like \(n \cdot 0 = 0\). A pair would need a value of \(\mathbf{0}\), so there are no such pairs, and the proofs have no cases.

\[ \begin{aligned} f &: \tau \times \mathbf{0} \to \mathbf{0} & f &= \lambda p{:}\tau \times \mathbf{0}.\, p.2 \\ g &: \mathbf{0} \to \tau \times \mathbf{0} & g &= \lambda z{:}\mathbf{0}.\, \mathsf{case}_{\mathbf{0}}\ z\ \{\} \end{aligned} \]

\(\tau_1 \times \tau_2 \cong \tau_2 \times \tau_1\)

Like \(n \cdot m = m \cdot n\). Both programs swap the pair, and swapping twice gives it back.

\[ \begin{aligned} f &: \tau_1 \times \tau_2 \to \tau_2 \times \tau_1 & f &= \lambda p{:}\tau_1 \times \tau_2.\, (p.2, p.1) \\ g &: \tau_2 \times \tau_1 \to \tau_1 \times \tau_2 & g &= \lambda p{:}\tau_2 \times \tau_1.\, (p.2, p.1) \end{aligned} \]

\(\tau_1 \times (\tau_2 + \tau_3) \cong \tau_1 \times \tau_2 + \tau_1 \times \tau_3\)

Like \(n(m + k) = nm + nk\). A pair whose second part is "a \(\tau_2\) or a \(\tau_3\)" is the same as "a pair with a \(\tau_2\), or a pair with a \(\tau_3\)".

\[ \begin{aligned} f &: \tau_1 \times (\tau_2 + \tau_3) \to \tau_1 \times \tau_2 + \tau_1 \times \tau_3 \\ f &= \lambda p{:}\tau_1 \times (\tau_2 + \tau_3).\, \mathsf{case}_+\ p.2\ \{\mathsf{in}_1\ y \to \mathsf{in}_1\ (p.1, y) \\ &\qquad\qquad \mid \mathsf{in}_2\ z \to \mathsf{in}_2\ (p.1, z)\} \\[6pt] g &: \tau_1 \times \tau_2 + \tau_1 \times \tau_3 \to \tau_1 \times (\tau_2 + \tau_3) \\ g &= \lambda s{:}\tau_1 \times \tau_2 + \tau_1 \times \tau_3.\, \mathsf{case}_+\ s\ \{\mathsf{in}_1\ q \to (q.1, \mathsf{in}_1\ q.2) \\ &\qquad\qquad \mid \mathsf{in}_2\ q \to (q.1, \mathsf{in}_2\ q.2)\} \end{aligned} \]

\((\tau_1 \times \tau_2 \to \tau_3) \cong (\tau_1 \to \tau_2 \to \tau_3)\)

Like \(c^{a \cdot b} = (c^b)^a\). A function of a pair is the same as a function that takes its two arguments one at a time. \(f\) is usually called \(\mathsf{curry}\), and \(g\) \(\mathsf{uncurry}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} f &: (\tau_1 \times \tau_2 \to \tau_3) \to (\tau_1 \to \tau_2 \to \tau_3) \\ f &= \lambda h{:}\tau_1 \times \tau_2 \to \tau_3.\, \lambda x{:}\tau_1.\, \lambda y{:}\tau_2.\, h\ (x, y) \\[6pt] g &: (\tau_1 \to \tau_2 \to \tau_3) \to (\tau_1 \times \tau_2 \to \tau_3) \\ g &= \lambda k{:}\tau_1 \to \tau_2 \to \tau_3.\, \lambda p{:}\tau_1 \times \tau_2.\, k\ p.1\ p.2 \end{aligned} \]

Here the values are functions, and two functions are equal when they agree on every input. Rocq can't prove that, so the code assumes it, as funext.

\(\mathsf{bool} \cong \mathbf{1} + \mathbf{1}\)

\(\mathsf{true}\) is \(\mathsf{in}_1\ ()\), \(\mathsf{false}\) is \(\mathsf{in}_2\ ()\), and \(\mathsf{if}\) is a case. So the lecture's language doesn't need \(\mathsf{bool}\) built in: sums give it to us.

\[ \begin{aligned} f &: \mathsf{bool} \to \mathbf{1} + \mathbf{1} & f &= \lambda b{:}\mathsf{bool}.\, \mathsf{if}\ b\ \mathsf{then}\ \mathsf{in}_1\ ()\ \mathsf{else}\ \mathsf{in}_2\ () \\ g &: \mathbf{1} + \mathbf{1} \to \mathsf{bool} & g &= \lambda s{:}\mathbf{1} + \mathbf{1}.\, \mathsf{case}_+\ s\ \{\mathsf{in}_1\ x \to \mathsf{true} \mid \mathsf{in}_2\ y \to \mathsf{false}\} \end{aligned} \]

\(\mathsf{option}\ \tau \cong \mathbf{1} + \tau\)

\(\mathsf{None}\) is \(\mathsf{in}_1\ ()\), and \(\mathsf{Some}\ x\) is \(\mathsf{in}_2\ x\): "nothing, or a \(\tau\)". Like \(\mathsf{bool}\), \(\mathsf{option}\) is Rocq's, not the lecture's, so it is taken apart with a Rocq match.

3. Types as propositions

Read a type as a proposition, and a program of that type as a proof of it. This is the Curry–Howard correspondence, between the simply typed λ-calculus and intuitionistic propositional logic:

typepropositionRocq
\(\mathbf{1}\)\(\top\)True
\(\mathbf{0}\)\(\bot\)False
\(\tau_1 \times \tau_2\)\(\varphi \wedge \psi \)φ /\ ψ
\(\tau_1 + \tau_2\)\(\varphi \vee \psi \)φ \/ ψ
\(\tau_1 \to \tau_2\)\(\varphi \Rightarrow \psi \)φ -> ψ

Erase the terms from a typing rule and you get a rule of logic. From easiest to hardest: \(()\) proves \(\top\); a case on \(\mathbf{0}\) is “from false, anything”; a pair is a proof of \(\varphi \wedge \psi\) from a proof of each; a case is a proof by cases; a \(\lambda\) proves \(\varphi \Rightarrow \psi\) by assuming \(\varphi\) and proving \(\psi\); and application is modus ponens:

\[\frac{}{\vdash () : \mathbf{1}}\]
\[\leadsto\quad \frac{}{\top}\]
\[\frac{\vdash e : \mathbf{0}}{\vdash \mathsf{case}_{\mathbf{0}}\ e\ \{\} : \tau}\]
\[\leadsto\quad \frac{\bot}{\xi}\]
\[\frac{\vdash e_1 : \tau_1 \quad \vdash e_2 : \tau_2}{\vdash (e_1, e_2) : \tau_1 \times \tau_2}\]
\[\leadsto\quad \frac{\varphi \quad \psi}{\varphi \wedge \psi}\]
\[\frac{\vdash e : \tau_1 \times \tau_2}{\vdash e.1 : \tau_1}\]
\[\leadsto\quad \frac{\varphi \wedge \psi}{\varphi}\]
\[\frac{\vdash e : \tau_1}{\vdash \mathsf{in}_1\ e : \tau_1 + \tau_2}\]
\[\leadsto\quad \frac{\varphi}{\varphi \vee \psi}\]
\[\frac{\vdash e : \tau_1 + \tau_2 \quad x_1{:}\tau_1 \vdash e_1 : \tau \quad x_2{:}\tau_2 \vdash e_2 : \tau}{\vdash \mathsf{case}_+\ e\ \{\mathsf{in}_1\ x_1 \to e_1 \mid \mathsf{in}_2\ x_2 \to e_2\} : \tau}\]
\[\leadsto\quad \frac{\varphi \vee \psi \quad \varphi \vdash \xi \quad \psi \vdash \xi}{\xi}\]
\[\frac{x{:}\tau_1 \vdash e : \tau_2}{\vdash \lambda x{:}\tau_1.\, e : \tau_1 \to \tau_2}\]
\[\leadsto\quad \frac{\varphi \vdash \psi}{\varphi \Rightarrow \psi}\]
\[\frac{\vdash e_1 : \tau_1 \to \tau_2 \quad \vdash e_2 : \tau_1}{\vdash e_1\, e_2 : \tau_2}\]
\[\leadsto\quad \frac{\varphi \Rightarrow \psi \quad \varphi}{\psi}\]

One proof, two readings

Take currying, \((\varphi \wedge \psi \Rightarrow \xi) \Rightarrow (\varphi \Rightarrow \psi \Rightarrow \xi)\). A leaf with no line above it, such as \(\Delta \vdash \varphi\), uses an assumption. Write \(\Delta\) for all three assumptions, \(\varphi \wedge \psi \Rightarrow \xi, \varphi, \psi\). Here is a proof tree using the rules above:

\[ \dfrac{\dfrac{\dfrac{\dfrac{\Delta \vdash \varphi \wedge \psi \Rightarrow \xi \quad \dfrac{\Delta \vdash \varphi \quad \Delta \vdash \psi}{\Delta \vdash \varphi \wedge \psi}{\scriptstyle\wedge\mathrm{I}}} {\Delta \vdash \xi}{\scriptstyle\Rightarrow\mathrm{E}}} {\varphi \wedge \psi \Rightarrow \xi, \varphi \vdash \psi \Rightarrow \xi}{\scriptstyle\Rightarrow\mathrm{I}}} {\varphi \wedge \psi \Rightarrow \xi \vdash \varphi \Rightarrow \psi \Rightarrow \xi}{\scriptstyle\Rightarrow\mathrm{I}}} {\vdash (\varphi \wedge \psi \Rightarrow \xi) \Rightarrow (\varphi \Rightarrow \psi \Rightarrow \xi)}{\scriptstyle\Rightarrow\mathrm{I}} \]

Now the same tree as a typing derivation. Each assumption gets a variable, and each line gets the program its rule builds. Write \(\sigma\) for \(\tau_1 \times \tau_2 \to \tau_3\), the type that \(\varphi \wedge \psi \Rightarrow \xi\) becomes, and \(\Gamma\) for \(h{:}\sigma, x{:}\tau_1, y{:}\tau_2\):

\[ \dfrac{\dfrac{\dfrac{\dfrac{\Gamma \vdash h : \sigma \quad \dfrac{\Gamma \vdash x : \tau_1 \quad \Gamma \vdash y : \tau_2}{\Gamma \vdash (x, y) : \tau_1 \times \tau_2}{\scriptstyle\wedge\mathrm{I}}} {\Gamma \vdash h\ (x, y) : \tau_3}{\scriptstyle\Rightarrow\mathrm{E}}} {h{:}\sigma, x{:}\tau_1 \vdash \lambda y{:}\tau_2.\, h\ (x, y) : \tau_2 \to \tau_3}{\scriptstyle\Rightarrow\mathrm{I}}} {h{:}\sigma \vdash \lambda x{:}\tau_1.\, \lambda y{:}\tau_2.\, h\ (x, y) : \tau_1 \to \tau_2 \to \tau_3}{\scriptstyle\Rightarrow\mathrm{I}}} {\vdash \lambda h{:}\sigma.\, \lambda x{:}\tau_1.\, \lambda y{:}\tau_2.\, h\ (x, y) : \sigma \to (\tau_1 \to \tau_2 \to \tau_3)}{\scriptstyle\Rightarrow\mathrm{I}} \]

Steps simplify proofs. The lecture's rule \((v_1, v_2).1 \longrightarrow v_1\) builds a pair and immediately takes it apart. As a proof, that is a detour: prove \(\varphi \wedge \psi \), only to use \(\varphi \). The step removes the detour, so evaluating a program is simplifying a proof.

Excluded middle

Can you prove \(\varphi \vee \neg \varphi \)?

Here \(\neg \varphi \) is \(\varphi \Rightarrow \bot\), so as a type it is \(\varphi + (\varphi \to \mathbf{0})\). A program of this type would have to choose \(\mathsf{in}_1\) or \(\mathsf{in}_2\) without knowing anything about \(\varphi \): \(\mathsf{in}_1\) needs a proof of \(\varphi \), and \(\mathsf{in}_2\) needs a way to turn any proof of \(\varphi \) into a contradiction. There is no such program, so \(\varphi \vee \neg \varphi \) can't be proved this way. That is exactly what makes the logic intuitionistic.

But it can't be refuted either: its double negation, \(\neg\neg(\varphi \vee \neg \varphi )\), has a proof. Given \(h : \neg(\varphi \vee \neg \varphi )\), choose the right side; to show \(\neg \varphi \), assume \(p : \varphi \); then \(\mathsf{in}_1\ p\) contradicts \(h\).
